What the bill does

A short summary of each section. The full text is on the Bill page.

  1. Section 1

    Funding Allotment

    Gives FEMA $1.75 billion a year for local grants and NOAA $250 million a year for early warning, paid for with revenue from energy production on federal lands and waters. It creates no new taxes.

  2. Section 2

    Climate Ready Communities Grants

    Pays for projects like cooling centers, shade trees, restored wetlands, raised homes, fire resistant roofs, and water recycling. Communities need a local adaptation plan, and at least 40 percent of the money goes to small, rural, tribal, and low income communities.

  3. Section 3

    Early Warning Systems

    Keeps every National Weather Service forecast office staffed around the clock and pays for flood sirens, river gauges, and alert systems near campgrounds, youth camps, and rivers.

  4. Section 4

    Extreme Heat Protection

    Adds extreme heat to the Stafford Act so a deadly heat wave can be declared a major disaster, and requires each funded state to have a heat emergency plan.

  5. Section 5

    Accountability and Sunset Clause

    Requires yearly reports to Congress and a GAO audit every two years. The Act ends after five years unless Congress renews it.

Why I wrote this bill

I became interested in this topic through my AP Environmental Science passion project, Adaptation to Environmental Change. My group studied how extreme heat, wildfires, and flooding affect people and ecosystems. I learned that preparing for disasters before they happen is just as important as responding afterward, but not all communities have the resources to do so.

I wanted my bill to address this gap by combining federal support with local decision-making. The federal government would provide funding, research, and early warning systems, while communities would identify their own risks and choose projects that best meet their needs. My goal was to make disaster preparation more accessible and protect communities before disasters strike.

Shu Nishioka

A Bill to establish a Climate Ready Communities Program that helps American communities prepare for extreme heat, flooding, wildfire, and drought.

Be it enacted by the House of Representatives and the United States Senate in Congress assembled, that

Purpose & Goals

The bill aims to help American communities prepare for the effects of climate change before disasters strike. In 2025, the United States suffered 23 weather and climate disasters that each caused at least $1 billion in damage, and extreme heat is the deadliest weather hazard in the nation. Preparing ahead of time saves both lives and money, since every $1 the federal government spends on disaster mitigation saves $6 in future losses. The purpose of this legislation is to protect lives, save taxpayer money, and give local communities the funding and tools they need to adapt to extreme heat, flooding, wildfire, and drought.

Section 1: Funding Allotment

  1. A total of $10 billion, or $2 billion per year for five years, shall be provided to the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) to help communities prepare for climate disasters.
  2. The funds shall be distributed as follows:
    1. FEMA shall receive $1.75 billion per year for Climate Ready Communities Grants under Section 2.
    2. NOAA shall receive $250 million per year to improve early warning systems under Section 3.
  3. Funds shall be provided by revenue the federal government already collects from oil, gas, coal, and renewable energy production on federal lands and waters, so this Act creates no new taxes.

Section 2: Climate Ready Communities Grants

  1. FEMA shall award grants to states, tribes, and local governments for projects that protect people and property from climate disasters, such as cooling centers and shade trees for extreme heat, restored wetlands and raised homes for flooding, fire resistant roofs for wildfire, and water recycling for drought.
  2. To receive a grant, a community shall create a local climate adaptation plan that identifies its greatest climate risks and the residents most vulnerable to them.
  3. At least 40 percent of grant funds shall go to small, rural, tribal, and low income communities, and the federal government shall cover up to 90 percent of their project costs (75 percent for all other communities).

Section 3: Early Warning Systems

  1. NOAA shall fill vacant positions at the National Weather Service and keep every local forecast office staffed 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.
  2. NOAA shall award grants for flood warning sirens, river gauges, and emergency alert systems in areas at high risk of flash floods, with priority given to campgrounds, youth camps, and neighborhoods along rivers.

Section 4: Extreme Heat Protection

  1. The Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act shall be amended to add extreme heat to its list of major disasters, so the President may declare a deadly heat wave a major disaster and FEMA may help pay for cooling centers and emergency medical care.
  2. Each state that receives funds under this Act shall create a heat emergency plan that lists cooling center locations and explains how officials will check on elderly, disabled, and isolated residents during heat waves.

Section 5: Accountability and Sunset Clause

  1. FEMA and NOAA shall provide annual reports to Congress on how the funds under this Act were used, the projects completed, and the estimated damages avoided.
  2. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) shall audit the program every two years to ensure the funds are used efficiently and effectively.
  3. This Act shall remain in effect for five years from the date of enactment, after which it shall be subject to review and reauthorization by Congress.

Conclusion

Preparing American communities for extreme heat, flooding, wildfire, and drought before disasters strike would save lives, protect homes, and save taxpayer money. The Climate Ready Communities Act gives communities the funding, warning systems, and plans they need to stay safe as the climate changes.

The letter

My half page letter to Senator Alex Padilla of California, giving background on the problem and explaining why this bill is needed.

November 2, 2026

The Honorable Alex Padilla
United States Senate
Washington, DC 20510

Dear Senator Padilla,

I hope this letter finds you well. I am a high school student in California, and I am writing to ask you to support the Climate Ready Communities Act, a bill I wrote for my AP Government class after studying climate adaptation in AP Environmental Science.

In 2025, the United States experienced 23 weather and climate disasters that each caused at least $1 billion in damage. Californians saw this firsthand in January 2025, when the Palisades and Eaton fires killed 31 people and destroyed more than 16,000 homes and other buildings. Extreme heat is also a major concern, with 2,391 heat-related deaths reported nationwide in 2024. Meanwhile, federal support for preparing communities before disasters has been unreliable. FEMA canceled its main pre-disaster mitigation grant program in 2025, and a federal court later ruled that its cancellation was unlawful.

My bill would provide $2 billion per year for five years, funded by revenue the government already collects from energy production on federal lands and waters, without creating new taxes. The money would support local projects such as cooling centers, restored wetlands, and fire-resistant roofs, as well as flood warning sirens and fully staffed National Weather Service offices. It would also allow the President to declare a deadly heat wave a major disaster. According to the National Institute of Building Sciences, every $1 spent on federal disaster mitigation grants saves an estimated $6 in future costs. Preparing now can help communities avoid much greater expenses and losses later.

You can read the full bill and my sources on my website, linked below. I hope you will consider supporting legislation like this in the Senate. Thank you for taking the time to read my letter.

Sincerely,
Shu Nishioka
